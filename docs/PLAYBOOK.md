# Clickable Objects: A Teacher's Playbook for Online English Lessons

Sep 21, 2026 · @beersloth

## Why this changes an online lesson

An in-person lesson has three things in it: you, your student, and the objects between you. An online lesson has two. That missing third thing is why so much of what works in a classroom stops working on a video call.

Screen sharing is the usual workaround, and it costs you your face. The moment you share a slide, your student loses your mouth shape, your expression and the eye contact that makes a nervous learner willing to speak.

Clickable objects put the third thing back without taking the face away. Objects sit in the space between your video tiles. Both of you see them, touch them and move them, and your student sees you at the same time.

This matters more in language teaching than almost anywhere else. Children learn their first words through joint attention, which means an adult and a child looking at and pointing to the same object. Video calls switch that mechanism off; everything below is a way of switching it back on.

Read what follows as a toolkit rather than a script. Once you know what each object type does, you will invent better activities for your own students than anyone could write down for you.

## Your toolkit: ten kinds of object

Everything in this playbook is built from ten object types. Learn these and the rest of the document is mostly combinations of them.

| Object | What it does | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Draggable object and drop zone | Your student picks something up and puts it somewhere; the zone accepts or refuses it | Prepositions, sorting, categorising, timelines |
| Backdrop | A picture behind everything: a room, a street, a shop, a diagram | Scenes, roleplay, and anywhere the objects need a place to be |
| Reveal card | Tap once for the audio, twice for a picture, three times for the translation | Vocabulary, and helping a lost student without breaking English-only |
| Snap connector | Two pieces that join only when the combination is correct | Word order, collocations, phrasal verbs |
| Teacher panel | A private layer only you can see | Your prompts, this student's weak words, a quiet timer |
| Persistent object | Stays where you left it, week after week | Long-term vocabulary, spaced review, the memory-palace effect |
| Turn token | An object you throw at a student, or one they have to spend | Turn-taking, group classes, breaking silence |
| Confidence slider | Your student sets it privately; you see the result | Checking comprehension without asking out loud |
| Prop set | A themed group of objects with labels and audio | Roleplay: restaurant, airport, doctor, job interview |
| Dice and spinner | Randomises a prompt, a tense, a person or a topic | Warm-ups, drills, fluency practice |
| Timer and scoreboard | Counts down, counts up, keeps score | Games, timed speaking, team competition |

Mix them freely. Almost every strong activity below is two or three of these used together.

## Grammar made physical

Grammar that describes relationships in space has never been teachable on a flat screen. These are the points where objects help most.

**Prepositions of place.** Six objects on a shelf. You say "the keys are under the book" in English only and your student drags. A correct placement holds and the written sentence appears; a wrong one simply does not stick. You never say "no, that's wrong" out loud, which matters, because correction costs face and physics does not.

**Prepositions of movement.** In and into, on and onto, through, across, over, around. These need motion to make sense, and dragging is motion. Send the ball over the wall, through the door and around the tree, and the difference stops being a list to memorise.

**Articles.** Students whose first language has no articles find a and the almost invisible. Use two zones: new and known. Put a cup on the table and say "there is a cup"; move it into the known zone and it becomes "the cup". The moment it crosses is the rule.

**Word order.** Sentence pieces as blocks that refuse to sit in the wrong slot. This fixes adverb placement ("I usually go", never "I go usually"), adjective order ("a big old red Italian car") and question formation, where your student physically inverts two blocks and watches a statement become a question.

**Tenses on a timeline.** A line across the space: past, now, future. Students drag sentences onto the position the tense implies. Present perfect is the classic disaster and the classic win, because "I have lived here for five years" is an object that stretches from the past to now, and once they see that shape it stops competing with the past simple.

**Countable and uncountable.** Two containers: a jar that pours and a box that holds separate things. Rice, money and information go in the jar; coins, notes and facts go in the box. Much and many, few and little, and "a piece of" all fall out of the same picture.

**Comparatives and superlatives.** Objects with a visible dimension: three houses of different sizes, four people of different heights. Line them up and produce the sentence. Place two side by side instead of in a row to get "not as tall as".

**Conditionals.** A real zone and an unreal zone, with a third for the past that never happened. Drag the situation into a zone and the tense follows from where it landed. The hardest thing about the third conditional is that it describes something impossible, and a zone marked impossible does that work for you.

**Phrasal verbs.** The verb is one block, the particle another. Separable and inseparable becomes literal: "turn the light off" lets the object slide between the pieces, while "look after the children" does not, because those pieces are welded. Then sort twenty phrasal verbs by particle and your student discovers that up often means finished and out often means away.

**Reported speech.** Two panels: what was said, and what you now report. Move the sentence across the gap and every element steps back one place, visibly. Students who cannot recall the rule can usually recall the direction.

## Vocabulary that sticks

**The reveal ladder.** Each vocabulary object gives help in stages: audio first, then a picture, then the translation. Your student takes only as much help as they need, silently, so you never stop speaking English to rescue anyone. Explain the ladder once in the first lesson and then never mention it again.

**The room as a memory palace.** Leave objects where you put them. Kitchen words on the left, travel words on the right, work words above. Where a word lives is a far stronger memory cue than where it sits in a list, and until now an online lesson gave you nowhere to put anything.

**Spaced review.** Words your student is forgetting fade; words due for review glow. Open the lesson by asking them to find the three faded objects and use each one in a sentence. Three minutes, no preparation, and it hits exactly the words that are slipping.

**Collocations and chunks.** Vocabulary is not single words, it is the company words keep. Snap connectors drill the pairs that never stop causing trouble: make and do, say and tell, take and have. Store whole phrases as single objects too, because fluency comes from retrieving "would you mind" or "I'm afraid I can't" in one piece rather than building it from parts.

**Word families.** One root in the centre, its forms around it: decide, decision, decisive, decisively. Give your student a sentence with a gap and let them drag in the form that fits. This is the single highest-value activity for exam students, because it is most of the Cambridge use-of-English paper.

**Grading and shades of meaning.** Draw a line and have them place words along it: cool, warm, hot; annoyed, angry, furious; like, love, adore. Learners collect synonyms and treat them as interchangeable. A line forces them to commit to a difference.

**Personal object sets.** Build sets from your student's real life: their actual job, their actual kitchen, their actual commute. It takes two minutes to assemble and it outperforms any coursebook set, because the words are ones they will need this week.

## Getting quiet students to speak

One rule before the techniques: whoever moves the objects is the one doing the talking. If you hold the objects, your own talking time goes up and your student's goes down. Hand them over.

**Describe and arrange.** You hold a picture your student cannot see; they hold the objects. They must describe what they think you have, and you place the objects exactly as described. When it comes out wrong, the English was wrong, and neither of you has to say so. This is the best speaking activity in the whole playbook and it needs nothing but a set of objects and your silence.

**Information gap.** Because each of you can be shown different objects, every classic gap task now works online. Spot the difference between two sets. Complete a timetable where you hold the mornings and they hold the afternoons. Find out which of six flats they should rent when only you know the rents.

**Something to look at.** Shy learners speak more when there is a third thing in the room, because it takes the pressure off looking at a face. Put an object between you and ask about that rather than about them. "Tell me about this photo" gets four times the words that "tell me about your weekend" does.

**Turn tokens.** In a group, give each student three tokens they must spend before the end. Nobody dominates, nobody hides, and you stop having to cold-call. In a one-to-one, give yourself only five tokens and see how little you can say.

**Throwing the floor.** Toss an object onto a student's tile to pass them the turn. It is playful in a way that saying their name never is, and it removes the small jolt of being picked on.

**Roleplay with props.** A menu, a table, a till, a departure board. Learners who freeze at "imagine you are in a restaurant" often unfreeze the moment there is a menu they can pick up. Give them the props and take the waiter's part yourself, or let them run both sides while you say nothing.

**The confidence slider.** Ask your student to set it privately every ten minutes. You will discover that the lesson you thought was going well was moving too fast, which is something no student has ever volunteered out loud.

**A channel for the ones who will not speak yet.** Beginners and anxious learners can answer by moving an object long before they can answer in words. Let them. Comprehension shown physically is still comprehension, and it buys them the weeks they need.

## Pronunciation, stress and rhythm

None of these need the software to listen. You are the ear; the objects are the feedback, which is faster and gentler than saying the word again yourself.

**Minimal pairs.** Put two objects up: ship and sheep, bad and bed, live and leave, thin and tin, work and walk. Your student says one, you tap the one you actually heard. No discussion, no argument, no embarrassment, and about twenty repetitions a minute. Then swap roles so they have to hear the difference as well as make it.

**Word stress.** Big objects for stressed syllables, small ones for unstressed. Your student builds the shape of the word before saying it. Run PHOtograph, phoTOgraphy and photoGRAPHic one after another and the moving stress stops being a surprise.

**Sentence rhythm.** English is stress-timed, and most learners come from languages that give every syllable equal weight. Lay heavy objects on the content words and nothing on the function words, then have them say the sentence with only the heavy beats on time. It sounds wrong to them and right to everybody else, which is the whole lesson.

**Connected speech.** Push two objects together until they fuse: want to becomes wanna, did you becomes didja, a cup of becomes a cuppa. Learners are not failing to hear fast English, they are failing to hear that the pieces have joined. Show the joining.

**Intonation.** Drag the end of a sentence up or down and say it both ways. Rising for a question, falling for a statement, and the half-rise that makes a request polite instead of rude, which matters more in business English than any grammar point.

**The moving spotlight.** Write "I didn't say he stole the money" and let your student slide a spotlight along it, explaining what it means at each position. Seven positions, seven different meanings, one sentence. Nothing teaches contrastive stress faster.

**Sound groups instead of spelling.** Sort a pile of words by the vowel sound rather than the letters: through, blue, shoe and two in one bin. English spelling is not a system your student can trust, and sorting by sound is how they stop trying.

## The games bank

Ordered from quickest to longest. Everything here works one-to-one unless the row says otherwise, and most work better in a group.

| Game | How it runs | Drills | Minutes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Find the faded three | Student hunts the three faded objects from last week and uses each in a sentence | Recycled vocabulary | 3 |
| Odd one out | Four objects; remove the one that does not belong and justify it | Categories, because, justification | 5 |
| Object roulette | Spinner picks an object; talk about it for 30 seconds without stopping | Fluency, hesitation control | 5 |
| Dice story | Roll for a tense, a person and a place; produce one correct sentence | Tense accuracy under pressure | 5 |
| What's missing | Show eight objects, student looks away, remove one | Vocabulary recall, there was | 5 |
| Kim's game | Twelve objects for thirty seconds, then recall as many as possible | Vocabulary, plurals | 5 |
| Simon says | Move the objects exactly as instructed, getting faster | Imperatives, listening at speed | 5 |
| Twenty questions | You hide an object; student narrows it down with yes/no questions only | Question formation | 8 |
| Hot seat | One partner cannot see the object and must be talked to it without the word | Paraphrase and definition, the top exam skill | 8 |
| Guess Who | A grid of characters; ask questions and eliminate until one is left | Descriptions, has got, is wearing | 10 |
| Pairs | Face-down cards matched in pairs: word to picture, verb to particle, word to definition | Whatever you load into it | 10 |
| I went to the market | Cumulative chain; each turn adds one object and repeats all the rest | Memory, articles, plurals | 10 |
| Category race | Against a timer, drag as many words as possible into the right bins | Fast recall, classification | 10 |
| Treasure hunt | You hide an object in a scene; student locates it by asking | Prepositions of place | 10 |
| Battleship | Grid with coordinates; call shots, sink ships | Letters, numbers, listening accuracy | 12 |
| Spot the difference | Two versions of the same room, side by side, find every difference | Comparatives, there is and there are | 12 |
| Bingo | Words on a card; you read definitions rather than words | Listening, definitions | 12 |
| Auction | Teams are given money and bid on sentences, buying only the correct ones | Error correction, and it is ruthless fun | 15 |
| Story cubes | Roll five random objects and build one story containing all of them | Narrative tenses, linkers | 15 |
| Board game | Objects as squares; land on one and answer its prompt | Mixed review | 15 |
| Alibi | Pair invents an alibi, then is questioned separately; contradictions convict them | Past simple and continuous | 20 |
| Escape room | Five locked stages, each opened by producing correct English | Anything, and teenagers will not notice the time | 30 |

## Group classes, pairs and teams

**Everybody answers at once.** In a normal online class one student answers and the other seven listen. Give each student their own copy of the objects and all eight answer simultaneously, while you watch every answer appear. That is eight times the practice from the same question, and it is the strongest reason to use this with a group.

**Pair work without breakout rooms.** Give each pair their own private set of objects on the same call. Nobody moves anywhere, nobody is lost for four minutes, and you can see every pair at once instead of being trapped in one room while the others drift. You can also drop a hint into one pair's space without interrupting anybody else.

**Decide who can move what.** Lock the objects to one student for a turn-based activity, or unlock them for everyone when you want productive chaos. A dominant student is easier to handle by taking the objects away for two minutes than by saying anything.

**Teams and scoring.** Two teams, one scoreboard, a timer. Competition raises volume and lowers self-consciousness, which is usually what a quiet group needs. Keep rounds short and let the losing team start the next one.

**Silent voting.** Ask a question and have everyone place their token on an answer at the same moment. Nobody copies the confident student, and you see the real distribution of understanding rather than the loudest opinion.

**Turn tokens for the group.** Three tokens each, all of them spent before the end. It solves the two problems of every group class at once: the student who never stops and the student who never starts.

## Teaching young learners

Children cannot sit still through a video call, and no amount of enthusiasm fixes that. Giving them something to do with their hands is the whole solution, and it is the reason this works better with children than with anyone else.

**Physical response instead of answers.** Say it, they move it. "Put the elephant in the box." "Give the red one to me." "Make the dog jump." A four-year-old who cannot yet produce a sentence can prove they understood twenty of them, and that comprehension is where their English actually starts.
